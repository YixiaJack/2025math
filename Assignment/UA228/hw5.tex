\textbf{(a) Compute $u(\phi)$.}

At the equator ($\phi = 0$), the radius from Earth's axis is $r(0) = R_E$ (Earth's radius), and $u(0) = 0$. The absolute velocity at the equator is:
\[
u_a(0) = \Omega R_E + 0 = \Omega R_E
\]

The angular momentum at the equator is:
\[
L = m \cdot u_a(0) \cdot r(0) = m \Omega R_E \cdot R_E = m \Omega R_E^2
\]

At latitude $\phi$, the distance from Earth's axis is $r(\phi) = R_E \cos\phi$. By conservation of angular momentum:
\[
m(\Omega r(\phi) + u(\phi)) r(\phi) = m \Omega R_E^2
\]
\[
(\Omega R_E \cos\phi + u(\phi)) R_E \cos\phi = \Omega R_E^2
\]
\[
\Omega R_E \cos\phi + u(\phi) = \frac{\Omega R_E}{\cos\phi}
\]
\[
u(\phi) = \Omega R_E \left(\frac{1}{\cos\phi} - \cos\phi\right) = \Omega R_E \left(\frac{1 - \cos^2\phi}{\cos\phi}\right)
\]
\[
{u(\phi) = \Omega R_E \frac{\sin^2\phi}{\cos\phi}}
\]

Using $\Omega = 7.292 \times 10^{-5}$ rad/s and $R_E = 6.371 \times 10^6$ m:
\[
\Omega R_E = 7.292 \times 10^{-5} \times 6.371 \times 10^6 \approx 464.5 \text{ m/s}
\]

\textbf{(b) Latitude where $u(\phi) = 343$ m/s (speed of sound):}

\[
343 = 464.5 \cdot \frac{\sin^2\phi}{\cos\phi}

\[
\frac{\sin^2\phi}{\cos\phi} = \frac{343}{464.5} \approx 0.7384

\[
\frac{1 - c^2}{c} = 0.7384

\[
1 - c^2 = 0.7384c

\[
c^2 + 0.7384c - 1 = 0

Using the quadratic formula:
\[
c = \frac{-0.7384 + \sqrt{0.7384^2 + 4}}{2} = \frac{-0.7384 + \sqrt{4.5452}}{2} = \frac{-0.7384 + 2.132}{2} \approx 0.697

(2)
From the simplified meridional momentum equation:
\[
\frac{\partial v}{\partial t} + fu = C
\]

As the plane moves northward, it gains eastward velocity $u$ (from Problem 1). The Coriolis term $fu$ grows as both $f = 2\Omega\sin\phi$ and $u(\phi)$ increase with latitude.

The engine provides a constant northward acceleration $C$. However, the Coriolis term $fu$ acts to oppose the northward motion (it deflects eastward-moving air to the right in the Northern Hemisphere, which for a northward-moving parcel means opposing the northward motion).

Eventually, when $fu = C$, we have:
\[
\frac{\partial v}{\partial t} = C - fu = 0
\]

At this point, the plane cannot accelerate northward anymore. As $u$ continues to grow (if the plane keeps moving north), $fu > C$, which means $\frac{\partial v}{\partial t} < 0$, and the plane decelerates until $v = 0$.

\textbf{Conclusion:} The Coriolis force eventually balances the engine thrust, preventing further northward acceleration, and the plane stops moving northward.

The rate of change of mass equals the net mass flux into the box:
\[
\frac{\partial M}{\partial t} = \frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} \delta x \, \delta y \, \delta z

Mass flux through the $x$-faces:
\[
\text{In at } x - \frac{\delta x}{2}: \quad \rho u \Big|_{x-\frac{\delta x}{2}} \delta y \, \delta z

\[
\text{Out at } x + \frac{\delta x}{2}: \quad \rho u \Big|_{x+\frac{\delta x}{2}} \delta y \, \delta z
\]

Net flux in $x$-direction:
\[
\left(\rho u \Big|_{x-\frac{\delta x}{2}} - \rho u \Big|_{x+\frac{\delta x}{2}}\right) \delta y \, \delta z \approx -\frac{\partial(\rho u)}{\partial x} \delta x \, \delta y \, \delta z

Similarly for $y$ and $z$ directions. Total:
\[
\frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} \delta x \, \delta y \, \delta z = -\left(\frac{\partial(\rho u)}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial(\rho v)}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial(\rho w)}{\partial z}\right) \delta x \, \delta y \, \delta z

\[

\[
\frac{\text{Nonlinear}}{\text{Coriolis}} = \frac{U^2/L}{fU} = \boxed{\frac{U}{fL} \equiv Ro}
\]

\textbf{(c)} With $U = 10$ m/s, $L = 10^6$ m:

\begin{itemize}
\item[(i)] Equator: $f = 0$, so $Ro = \infty$ (undefined)
\item[(ii)] $30^\circ$N: $Ro = \frac{10}{7.27 \times 10^{-5} \times 10^6} = \frac{10}{72.7} \approx 0.14$
\item[(iii)] $45^\circ$N: $Ro = \frac{10}{1.03 \times 10^{-4} \times 10^6} = \frac{10}{103} \approx 0.097$
\item[(iv)] Pole: $Ro = \frac{10}{1.45 \times 10^{-4} \times 10^6} = \frac{10}{145} \approx 0.069$
\end{itemize}

\textbf{(d)} The Rossby number is small ($Ro \ll 1$) for latitudes away from the equator, roughly for $\phi \gtrsim 30^\circ$. At these latitudes, the Coriolis force dominates over nonlinear terms.

\item We are almost to the finish line, but what do we do with the first term in the momentum equation, $\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}$? We know that $u$ scales as $U$, but how does time $t$ scale? A reasonable way to make progress is to assume that time scales as $T$, the time it takes the a flow with speed $U$ to cross the characteristic length scale $L$, or
\[
T \approx \frac{L}{U}
\]
Using this scaling, show that $\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}$ scales the same as the nonlinear terms: $U^2/L$. This means that when the Rossby number is small, we can ignore the time derivative, too!


(8)
With $t = T\hat{t}$ and $T = L/U$:
\[
\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} \sim \frac{U}{T} = \frac{U}{L/U} = \frac{U^2}{L}
\]

This scales the same as the nonlinear terms: $\boxed{\frac{U^2}{L}}$.

When $Ro \ll 1$, both the time derivative and nonlinear terms are negligible compared to the Coriolis term, leaving a steady balance.

\textbf{(a)} This is called \textbf{geostrophic balance}.

\textbf{(b) Low pressure system (Northern Hemisphere):}

From geostrophic balance:
\[
fv = -\frac{1}{\rho}\frac{\partial p}{\partial x}, \quad -fu = -\frac{1}{\rho}\frac{\partial p}{\partial y}
\]

The pressure gradient points inward toward the low center. The Coriolis force must balance this, requiring flow perpendicular to the pressure gradient. In the Northern Hemisphere, winds blow \textbf{counterclockwise} around a low pressure system, with the low on the left of the wind direction.

\textbf{(c) High pressure system (Northern Hemisphere):}

The pressure gradient points outward from the high center. Winds blow \textbf{clockwise} around a high pressure system, with the high on the right of the wind direction.

\item Finally, use the thermal wind relationship to explain why there are westerly jets in both hemispheres. Please be quantitative, relating an estimate of the equator-to-pole temperature gradient to the strength of the jets.

(12)

The thermal wind relationship relates vertical wind shear to horizontal temperature gradients:
\[
\frac{\partial u_g}{\partial z} = -\frac{g}{fT}\frac{\partial T}{\partial y}
\]

or in pressure coordinates:
\[
\frac{\partial u_g}{\partial \ln p} = \frac{R}{f}\frac{\partial T}{\partial y}
\]

\textbf{Quantitative estimate:}

The equator-to-pole temperature difference is approximately $\Delta T \approx 50$ K over a distance $\Delta y \approx 10^7$ m (equator to pole).

Temperature gradient:
\[
\frac{\partial T}{\partial y} \approx -\frac{50}{10^7} = -5 \times 10^{-6} \text{ K/m}
\]
(negative because temperature decreases poleward)

At mid-latitudes ($\sim 45^\circ$), $f \approx 10^{-4}$ s$^{-1}$.

Integrating from surface to tropopause (scale height $H \approx 8$ km):
\[
\Delta u \approx -\frac{R}{f}\frac{\partial T}{\partial y} \cdot \ln\left(\frac{p_{surface}}{p_{tropopause}}\right)
\]
\[
\approx -\frac{287}{10^{-4}} \times (-5 \times 10^{-6}) \times \ln(10) \approx 2.87 \times 10^6 \times 5 \times 10^{-6} \times 2.3
\]
\[
\approx 33 \text{ m/s}
\]

\textbf{Explanation:}

In both hemispheres, temperature decreases from equator to pole. By thermal wind balance:
\begin{itemize}
\item Northern Hemisphere: $\frac{\partial T}{\partial y} < 0$ and $f > 0$, so $\frac{\partial u}{\partial z} > 0$ (westerly winds increase with height)
\item Southern Hemisphere: $\frac{\partial T}{\partial y} > 0$ (temperature increases toward pole, i.e., toward larger $y$ if $y$ points poleward) and $f < 0$, so $\frac{\partial u}{\partial z} > 0$ (also westerly)
\end{itemize}

Starting from weak surface winds, integration upward produces strong westerly jets at the tropopause ($\sim 10$ km altitude) in both hemispheres, with speeds of $\boxed{30-50 \text{ m/s}}$.
